# Behavioral Gaps — Supplement for Principal Interview

> 6 missing categories. Each entry: STAR framework, sample answer, Principal-level signal.

---

## 1. Delegation

**STAR:** Spring Boot 3 migration — I was the bottleneck across 6 teams. Built a tiered model: CI gates for mechanical changes, playbooks with exact migration steps, escalation criteria ("pull me in only if it touches payment Kafka or auth"). Trained 2 leads. Reviewed <10% of PRs. Migration done in 3 months (vs 6-month target).

**Answer:** "Delegation is a throughput problem, not a trust problem. I built systems — playbooks, gates, escalation criteria — so my team could execute without me. The Principal insight: know what only you can decide; everything else is a scaling problem."

**Signal:** *"I think in systems, not heroics. I build playbooks to replace myself."*

---

## 2. Mentorship

**STAR:** At MSys, a strong engineer was invisible — passed over for promotion twice. Gave them high-visibility ownership (NetApp Selenium suite), made them present at leadership reviews, credited them publicly. Promoted to Senior within 6 months.

**Answer:** "Promotion is about being seen doing the work. An engineer I mentored was technically solid but invisible. I gave them the work, the stage, and the credit. They were promoted in 6 months. My framework: work + visibility + credit = career growth."

**Signal:** *"I create engineers who outgrow my team. Growing others is a measurable output."*

---

## 3. Ambiguity

**STAR:** MyDish platform had zero architecture practice. Leadership said "make it better" — no specifics. Mapped all services, identified top 3 problems, prioritized observability first. Built a 90-day roadmap. Within 3 months, every service had dashboards; within 6 months, API standards were adopted.

**Answer:** "I thrive in ambiguity. Leadership gave me a vague problem — I defined the solution. I mapped everything, prioritized, and delivered iteratively. Ambiguity isn't a blocker; it's the space where you create structure."

**Signal:** *"I don't need a spec. I take vague problems, define boundaries, prioritize, and deliver."*

---

## 4. Hiring

**STAR:** Staff engineer on hiring panel at Dish. Redesigned evaluation: ambiguity-testing system design, behavioral signal checklist (ownership of failure, crediting others), real code review exercise, debrief format scoring 3 dimensions (technical, collaboration, ownership) — discussed the LOWEST score.

**Answer:** "Technical skill is table stakes. I look for ownership, collaboration, and how candidates handle feedback. I introduced a code review exercise — a PR with intentional bugs — and watched how they communicated. Hired 6 engineers; 5 of 6 are still performing well."

**Signal:** *"I treat hiring as a system design problem. The real signal is ownership and collaboration, not coding ability."*

---

## 5. Cross-Team Collaboration

**STAR:** Built Dynatrace/Snyk MCP servers, needed 5+ teams to adopt without authority. Didn't pitch — invited one engineer to shadow. Documented runnable playbooks, held open office hours, let teams customize. 5+ teams adopted within 3 months.

**Answer:** "Influence without authority is a product problem. I made it easy, documented everything as runnable playbooks, and let peer adoption drive itself. Within 3 months, 5+ teams adopted the tools. The Principal lesson: make the right thing the easy thing."

**Signal:** *"I don't need a title to drive change. I treat adoption as a product problem."*

---

## 6. Handling Failure

**STAR:** Rails-to-Java migration — committed to a timeline with no ramp-up buffer. 3 months in, team was behind, morale dropping. Owned the mistake publicly: "This is my miss, not the team's." Rebaselined, protected the team, added 15% buffer to future estimates.

**Answer:** "My worst leadership failure was a planning mistake. I owned it publicly. The VP told me, 'I trust your estimates more now because you'll tell me when you're wrong.' That's the paradox: transparency about failure builds trust. The team hit the revised timeline."

**Signal:** *"I own planning failures publicly. Transparency builds more trust than hiding mistakes."*
