# 16. Translating Business Requirements Into Technical Solutions

**Target:** Principal Software Engineer V — The Standard (IBTE)
**Duration:** 75-85 seconds

---

## Question

**Interviewer:** "You've talked a lot about architecture and AI. But I want to see the other direction — when a product manager or business stakeholder comes to you with a requirement, what's your process for turning that into a technical solution?"

---

## Answer

I don't start with technology. I start with understanding the problem.

First, I ask clarifying questions. What problem are we solving for the customer? What does success look like, and how do we measure it? What are the constraints, like timeline, budget, or compliance? I want to make sure we're solving the right problem, not just the stated requirement.

For example, if a product manager says 'subscribers should see their bill within one second,' that sounds like a performance requirement. But the real question is what user experience we're trying to deliver. Maybe the goal is reducing support calls for billing inquiries, not just making the page load faster. That changes the solution entirely.

Once I understand the problem, I map it to technical constraints. 'Subscribers see their bill in one second' means p99 latency under a thousand milliseconds, a caching layer, optimized queries, and a CDN for static content. 'Payment processing must never lose a transaction' means exactly once Kafka semantics, the outbox pattern, idempotent APIs, and dead letter queues.

Then I propose options with trade offs. Option A is quick, two sprints, but limited in scalability. Option B is more robust, four sprints, with full scalability. I recommend the one that fits the business context. If growth projections show we'll hit Option A's limits in six months, I recommend B and explain why the extra investment is worth it.

I validate with stakeholders. I share an architecture diagram, walk through the user flows, identify risks and mitigations, and agree on success metrics. Alignment before implementation.

And throughout the project, I communicate in business terms. I don't say 'we deployed three microservices with Kafka integration.' I say 'subscribers can now see real time bill updates, and we're measuring two hundred milliseconds average response time.'

## Structure

| Section | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | I don't start with technology — understand the problem first | ~5 sec |
| Clarify | Ask what problem, what success, what constraints | ~10 sec |
| Reframe example | "Bill in 1 second" → maybe it's about support calls, not speed | ~15 sec |
| Map to tech | Business requirement → p99, caching, outbox, idempotency | ~15 sec |
| Options | A (fast, limited) vs B (robust) — recommend with data | ~15 sec |
| Validate | Diagram, flows, risks, agreed metrics | ~10 sec |
| Close | Communicate in business terms | ~10 sec |

## Key Signals

- **Problem-first mindset:** "I don't start with technology" — the reframe
- **Business fluency:** Maps a product phrase to concrete technical constraints AND back to business value
- **Trade-off thinking:** Options with costs, risks, and a data-backed recommendation
- **Stakeholder collaboration:** Diagram walkthrough, agreed success metrics — not a handoff
- **Bilingual communication:** "Real time bill updates" not "three microservices with Kafka"

## Delivery Tips

- **"I don't start with technology"** — the whole answer in one line. Say it first, say it plainly
- **The bill example** — the reframe ("maybe it's about reducing support calls, not page speed") is the part that shows real stakeholder maturity. Don't rush it
- **The closing contrast** — "real time bill updates" vs "three microservices with Kafka" — this is the thesis. Let it land
- **The mapping list** — "p99 under a thousand milliseconds, caching, CDN" — say it as one breath, not a recital. It shows fluency, not memorization

## Follow-Up Questions

- **"What if the business pushes back on Option B's timeline?"** — I don't fight the timeline, I reframe the risk. 'We can ship Option A in two sprints, but growth projections show we hit its limits in six months, and the migration cost then is three times what it costs now.' The decision becomes about when they want to pay, not whether. I also look for a phased middle path — ship A's scope with B's architecture seams, so the upgrade is incremental.
- **"How do you handle vague requirements?"** — I don't accept them silently. I write down what I think the requirement means, including the assumptions, and send it back for confirmation. Written assumptions surface disagreements in a day; built assumptions surface them in production. Same discipline as spec driven development — the spec is the contract, and ambiguity is resolved before code, not after.
- **"What's an example where the stated requirement was wrong?"** — The bill example is real for me. The request was 'make billing load faster.' The actual problem was support calls — customers couldn't understand their bills, so they called. We fixed the bill layout and the explanations, added a self-service FAQ, and support calls dropped. The page speed was already fine. That's why clarifying questions come first — the stated problem is rarely the real problem.

## Notes

- This is a **process/framework** question, not a STAR story. The interviewer wants your methodology, not an anecdote — but keep the bill example ready as the concrete anchor
- The spec-driven development tie-in (in the vague-requirements follow-up) connects this answer to your AI SDLC story — same discipline, applied to humans and agents alike
- If they ask "give me a real example," the billing example works, or pivot to the Spring Boot 3 migration as the big-scale instance of requirement → spec → delivery
- Avoid jargon density: the interviewer may be a manager or architect. The two-direction translation (business → tech → business) is the skill being tested, so show it, don't just describe it
