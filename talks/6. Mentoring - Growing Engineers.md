# 6. Mentoring — Growing an Engineer From Mid-Level to Senior

**Target:** Principal Software Engineer V — The Standard (IBTE)
**Duration:** 70-80 seconds

---

## Question

**Interviewer:** "You've talked a lot about architecture and automation. But Principal engineers also grow the people around them. Can you tell me about a specific time you mentored someone and saw real growth?"

---

## Answer

There was a mid level engineer on my team at Dish who was technically solid but struggled with system thinking. He could implement a feature well, but he didn't see how his service fit into the bigger picture. He'd design APIs that worked in isolation but caused integration pain for downstream services.

I paired with him on architecture reviews. Instead of giving him the answers, I asked guided questions — why is this endpoint here, what happens if this service goes down, how would you scale this. I had him present his designs and I challenged his assumptions.

I also gave him progressively larger ownership. First a single API endpoint. Then a whole service. Then a cross service integration that required Kafka and a state machine. Each step, I reviewed less and let him own more.

Within 8 months, he independently architected and delivered the STB Health Monitor service — a DMZ gateway plus an internal service, deployed on Kubernetes with Kafka integration, full observability, and CI/CD pipelines. He designed it, built it, and deployed it with minimal oversight from me.

He was promoted from mid level to senior. More importantly, he started mentoring others on his team. That's the multiplier effect I look for — not just growing one person, but enabling them to grow others.

## Delivery Tips

- Start with the problem — "struggled with system thinking" gives context for why the mentorship was needed
- "I asked guided questions" — this is what separates good mentors. Say it with emphasis
- "He was promoted" — that's the closing proof. Let it land without extra commentary
- The last line "That's the multiplier effect" — this is the Principal-level insight. Pause before it

---

## Follow-Up Questions

- **"What was the hardest part of mentoring him?"** — Letting him fail safely. I had to stop myself from jumping in to fix his designs. The growth happened when he made mistakes and figured out the fix himself.
- **"How do you find time to mentor as a Staff Engineer?"** — I treat it as part of my job, not an add-on. Architecture reviews are a natural mentoring opportunity. I don't separate mentoring from technical work.
- **"What's the biggest mistake you've made as a mentor?"** — Early on, I gave too much guidance. I realized I was creating dependency, not independence. Now I hold back and let them struggle productively.
- **"Have you mentored more than one person at a time?"** — Yes, through the architecture guild. It's a force multiplier — one session helps 5-6 engineers at once, and they learn from each other's questions.

---

## Notes

- "Works in isolation" means the code works when tested alone, but causes problems when connected to real systems. The engineer didn't think about downstream consumers.
- STB = Set-Top Box (the cable box in customers' homes). STB Health Monitor monitors whether those devices are healthy.
- DMZ gateway = a security layer — a middleman that external devices talk to before reaching internal systems.
- This story pairs well with the org adoption story — both show you grow people and systems.
