# 12. Handling a Resistant Team

**Target:** Principal Software Engineer V — The Standard (IBTE)
**Duration:** 55-65 seconds

---

## Question

**Interviewer:** "You mentioned that you don't mandate adoption — you make things visible and teams pull it themselves. But what about the team that still says no? The one that's skeptical or just too busy. How do you handle that?"

---

## Answer

I start by understanding the resistance. Is it fear, workload, skepticism, or a valid technical objection?

If it's workload, I offer to implement it myself on one of their services. No training, no ramp up. I do the first one and they evaluate the result. Once they see reduced incidents or faster deployments, the resistance usually drops.

If it's skepticism, I find one or two enthusiastic engineers on that team. I help them succeed quietly. Within a few weeks, they become internal advocates. Peer influence is stronger than anything I can say.

If it's a valid technical objection — maybe the pattern genuinely doesn't fit their domain — I accept the exception and document it. Not everything needs to be uniform. Consistency has value but not infinite value.

And if it's none of those and the team is simply unwilling, I stay patient. Some teams need to feel the pain first. When they have an incident that the practice would have prevented, I revisit the conversation. By then, the data speaks for itself.

---

## Structure

| Section | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Diagnose the resistance first | ~5 sec |
| Workload | Offer to implement it myself, let results speak | ~15 sec |
| Skepticism | Find champions, enable them quietly | ~10 sec |
| Valid objection | Accept exception, document it | ~10 sec |
| Stubborn | Stay patient, revisit after an incident | ~10 sec |

## Key Signals

- **Diagnose first:** "Is it fear, workload, skepticism, or valid?" — you don't assume the reason
- **Empathy:** "If they're busy, I do the work myself" — you absorb the cost, don't push it on them
- **Champion strategy:** "Find 1-2 enthusiasts, help them succeed" — leverages peer influence over authority
- **Graceful exceptions:** "Accept valid exceptions, document them" — not dogmatic about uniformity
- **Patience:** "Some teams need to feel the pain first" — you don't escalate or complain

## Delivery Tips

- **"I start by understanding the resistance"** — this is the key opener. It shows empathy, not ego. Say it plainly
- **"I do the first one myself"** — this is your strongest signal. You're willing to do the work, not just delegate
- **"Not everything needs to be uniform"** — shows maturity. A Principal picks battles. Say it with conviction
- **The four categories** (fear, workload, skepticism, valid) — say them as a list, not a ramble. It shows you have a framework

## Follow-Up Questions

- **"What if the team is still resistant even after you show results?"** — I ask myself honestly: is the pattern actually better for their use case, or am I pushing something that doesn't fit? If it genuinely fits and they're still resistant, I look for a proxy — a similar team that adopted it successfully. Peer pressure works better than me pushing.
- **"Have you ever been wrong about a pattern you pushed?"** — Yes. I pushed a shared caching library early on that turned out to be over-engineered for most teams. The right answer was simpler per-service caching. I learned: don't abstract until you've seen the pattern three times. Premature shared libraries create more coupling than value.

## Notes

- This is a "process/framework" question, not a STAR story. The interviewer wants to see your approach to influence, not a specific anecdote
- You can pivot to the AI spec-driven adoption story (question 4) as a real example of this framework in action
- The "find champions" tactic is the most important — it shows you understand how change actually happens in organizations
- If they want a specific example of a valid exception, mention a team that had a legacy system where the new pattern would have required a full rewrite — you documented the exception and revisited after the rewrite was scheduled
