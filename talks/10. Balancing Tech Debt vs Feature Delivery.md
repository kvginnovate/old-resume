# 10. Balancing Technical Debt vs Feature Delivery

**Target:** Principal Software Engineer V — The Standard (IBTE)
**Duration:** 75-85 seconds

---

## Question

**Interviewer:** "You've led migrations, built new platforms, and driven standards. But you've also delivered features. How do you balance the tension between paying down technical debt and shipping new capabilities?"

---

## Answer

I don't see it as a binary choice. I use a framework that classifies tech debt by business impact, and that determines the response.

First, I classify the debt into four categories. Security debt — like an end of life framework or a known CVE — is non negotiable. It gets fixed immediately because the cost of a breach is higher than any feature delay. Scaling debt — where the system will break under projected growth — gets fixed before it blocks us. Developer productivity debt, like slow builds or manual deployment steps, gets a quarterly budget. And aesthetic debt, like code that's ugly but works, goes to the backlog.

I make tech debt visible. We track it in the backlog, tagged and estimated. I show the correlation between debt and incidents — when we had no circuit breakers, every downstream failure caused a P0. That data makes the business case obvious.

I also negotiate a budget. I work with Product to allocate 20 percent of sprint capacity for platform health. That's not a surprise — it's a committed agreement. If a feature touches a debt area, I include remediation in the feature estimate. And we do quarterly hardening sprints for larger items.

But I also speak in business terms. I don't say 'we need to refactor the billing service.' I say 'the billing service has a 30 percent error rate during peak traffic, and fixing it saves us 2 hours of on call time per week.' When you frame it as cost and risk, Product teams make better decisions.

The key is: if you only build features, velocity slows down over time as the system gets brittle. If you only fix debt, you never ship anything. The 80-20 balance — 80 percent features, 20 percent platform health — keeps the system healthy without stopping delivery.

## Structure

| Section | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Not a binary choice — use a framework | ~5 sec |
| Classification | 4 categories: security, scaling, productivity, aesthetic | ~15 sec |
| Visibility | Backlog tracking, correlation between debt and incidents | ~10 sec |
| Budget | 20% sprint capacity, quarterly hardening sprints, feature tax | ~20 sec |
| Business framing | "30% error rate" not "refactor the billing service" | ~10 sec |
| Closing | 80-20 balance — both matter | ~10 sec |

## Key Signals

- **Framework, not opinion:** "Four categories with different responses" — structured thinking
- **Prioritization by impact:** Security is non-negotiable, aesthetic is backlog — shows judgment
- **Data-driven:** "Correlation between debt and incidents" — not emotional pleading
- **Negotiation skill:** "20% sprint capacity, negotiated with Product" — you collaborate, not demand
- **Business communication:** "Speak in business terms" — translates technical to business value
- **Balance:** "80-20 keeps the system healthy without stopping delivery"

## Delivery Tips

- **The four categories** — say them deliberately. Security, scaling, productivity, aesthetic. They show you've thought about this systematically
- **"I don't say 'refactor the billing service'"** — the contrast between technical and business framing shows maturity. Emphasize it
- **"The key is"** — pause before the closing. The 80-20 balance is the thesis. Let it land
- **Keep it conversational** — this is a process question, not a story. Don't rush. It should feel like you're explaining your philosophy, not reciting

## Follow-Up Questions

- **"What if Product refuses the 20% budget?"** — I start with data. I show the incident correlation — how many P0s we had last quarter that trace back to tech debt. If that doesn't work, I propose a compromise: 10% for two sprints, then review. The goal is to start the habit, not win the argument in one meeting.

- **"How do you decide what counts as 'scaling debt' vs 'aesthetic debt'?"** — Scaling debt has a specific trigger: the system will break under projected growth within a quarter. If it's not going to break, it's productivity or aesthetic. I use traffic projections and capacity planning data to make the call.

- **"What's an example of a tech debt decision you made that you later regretted?"** — I once spent two sprints building a generic caching layer before we knew the actual caching patterns we needed. It was over-engineered and we rewrote it later. I learned: don't fix debt you don't have yet. Let the system tell you what hurts.

## Notes

- This is a "process/framework" question, not a STAR story. The interviewer is testing whether you have a systematic approach to trade-offs, not whether you can tell a specific anecdote
- The 80-20 split is a guideline, not a hard rule. If a crisis hits, you adjust. The flexibility is as important as the framework
- Use this when the interviewer asks about: trade-offs, balancing competing priorities, dealing with Product, technical debt strategy, platform health
- If they ask "give me a specific example" — pivot to the circuit breaker story (incident story) or the Spring Boot migration (cross-team initiative story) as evidence of the framework in action
