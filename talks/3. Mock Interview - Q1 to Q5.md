## Question 3: Tell Me About the Sling TV Modernization (UMS)

**Answer:**

> "The Sling User Management Service, or UMS, was built on 80 plus legacy Ruby on Rails APIs running on a monolith. It worked, but it was brittle. Every deployment was high risk, scaling meant vertical scaling, and hiring Rails developers was getting harder.
>
> I proposed the modernization. My role was architect and technical lead. I defined the target architecture, chose the technology stack, and designed the migration approach. I didn't manage the team, I led the technical direction.
>
> We decomposed the monolith into 6 bounded contexts using domain driven design. Billing, content catalog, entitlements, recommendations, device management, and support. Each context became its own microservice with its own database. Communication was event driven over Kafka, with REST for synchronous query flows.
>
> The hardest part was data consistency. When you split a monolith's database, you lose ACID transactions. We used the Saga pattern. Each service publishes an event when its local transaction completes, downstream services listen and update their own state, and we had compensation logic to roll back on failure. That took three iterations to get right.
>
> Validation was the second hardest problem. We ran old and new in parallel for two weeks. Feature flags routing 10 percent of traffic initially, then 50 50, then 100 percent, with the ability to roll back instantly. We had a data reconciliation job comparing responses from both systems for every request. If they diverged, we investigated before the next traffic increase.
>
> The result was 70 percent faster delivery per engineer. A service that took two weeks to build now takes three days."

**Key signals:**
- Agency: "I proposed" — not assigned
- Architecture ownership: "defined the target architecture"
- Hands-on credibility: "The Saga pattern took three iterations to get right"
- Validation discipline: feature flags, parallel run, data reconciliation
- Result: "70% faster delivery"

## Question 5: Tell Me About a Time You Mentored an Engineer Who Grew Significantly

**Answer:**

> "There was a mid level engineer on my team at Dish who was technically solid but struggled with system thinking. He could implement a feature well, but he didn't see how his service fit into the bigger picture. He'd design APIs that worked in isolation but caused integration pain for downstream services.
>
> I paired with him on architecture reviews. Instead of giving him the answers, I asked guided questions — why is this endpoint here, what happens if this service goes down, how would you scale this. I had him present his designs and I challenged his assumptions.
>
> I also gave him progressively larger ownership. First a single API endpoint. Then a whole service. Then a cross service integration that required Kafka and a state machine. Each step, I reviewed less and let him own more.
>
> Within 8 months, he independently architected and delivered the STB Health Monitor service — a DMZ gateway plus an internal service, deployed on Kubernetes with Kafka integration, full observability, and CI/CD pipelines. He designed it, built it, and deployed it with minimal oversight from me.
>
> He was promoted from mid level to senior. More importantly, he started mentoring others on his team. That's the multiplier effect I look for — not just growing one person, but enabling them to grow others."

**What "in isolation" means:** By itself, alone, not connected to other systems. The engineer's code worked when tested alone, but caused problems when other teams tried to use it because he didn't think about downstream consumers.

**What "system thinking" means:** Considering the whole picture — who calls this API, what do they need, what happens when it fails, how does it scale. Not just "does my endpoint return 200."

**Key signals:**
- People development: specific person, specific growth, specific timeline
- System thinking: you diagnose the gap and teach
- Progressive ownership: structured growth plan
- Letting go: "each step, I reviewed less"
- Multiplier effect: "he now mentors others"
- Concrete outcome: STB Health Monitor — named, shipped, real scope
