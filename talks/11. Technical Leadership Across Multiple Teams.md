# 11. Technical Leadership Across Multiple Engineering Teams

**Target:** Principal Software Engineer V — The Standard (IBTE)
**Duration:** 70-80 seconds

---

## Question

**Interviewer:** "You've described working across teams, defining standards, and getting adoption. But I want to hear specifically — how do you actually provide technical leadership when you don't manage any of those teams? What's your day to day?"

---

## Answer

I lead through three things. Architecture guilds, shared standards, and being hands on when it matters.

First, I run a weekly architecture sync across teams. Each team presents upcoming design decisions. I facilitate the discussion, ask questions, and share patterns we've used elsewhere. We document decisions as Architecture Decision Records so we don't relitigate the same topic six months later. It's not a gate, it's a collaborative discussion.

Second, I build shared standards. I maintain Spring Boot starter libraries with best practices baked in, CI/CD pipeline templates that teams can adopt, and API design guidelines covering OpenAPI standards, error formats, and pagination. I don't mandate them. I build the first template, use it on one service, and show the team how much faster integration is. Teams adopt it organically because they see the benefit.

Third, I'm still hands on. I review design docs across teams, I jump into team channels to help with tricky problems, and I pair program on complex implementations. Credibility comes from doing, not just directing. If I'm asking a team to use circuit breakers, they need to see I've implemented them myself.

I also make sure the team gets visibility. I highlight their wins in leadership meetings, I nominate engineers for awards when they apply patterns well, and I share knowledge through internal tech talks.

And I'm consistent without being rigid. I say 'here's the recommended pattern, and here's when it's okay to deviate.' I explain the why behind the standards so teams can make informed exceptions. If a team has a legitimate reason to do something differently, I support that. The goal is consistency, not conformity.

- **"I don't mandate"** — this is the recurring theme across all your answers. It's your philosophy. Say it consistently

## Follow-Up Questions

- **"How do you handle a team that refuses to follow the standards?"** — I start with a conversation. I ask what's driving their decision — is it a legitimate technical constraint, or is it just unfamiliarity? If it's a legitimate constraint, I support the exception and document it. If it's unfamiliarity, I pair with them on the first implementation. The goal is adoption, not compliance. I've never had to escalate.
- **"How do you keep the architecture guild from becoming a bottleneck?"** — Time box it to 30 minutes. No design review is blocked waiting for the guild. And if a team needs a fast decision, they can reach me directly. The guild is for shared learning, not approval.
- **"How do you measure the effectiveness of your technical leadership?"** — I look at three things: how many teams voluntarily adopt the standards, how many ADRs reference patterns from other teams, and how often I'm asked to review designs before implementation starts. Those are leading indicators that trust is building.

## Notes

- This is a "process/framework" question, not a STAR story. The interviewer wants to see that you have a systematic approach to leading without authority
- The three-part structure (guilds, standards, hands-on) is easy to remember and gives you a natural flow
- If they ask for a specific example of a standard you drove — pivot to the Spring Boot migration story (question 9) or the OpenAPI templates story as evidence
- The "consistency, not conformity" line is a discovery — it's a distinct Principal-level insight. Use it deliberately
